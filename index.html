Build log · 01

Ragnar — a voice assistant that never leaves the house

My first fully-local voice build. The honest version: what it does, and every wall I walked into getting there. The walls are the point.

Talk to it, it answers — and nothing you say ever touches the internet.

A little pyramid speaker on my desk hears me, a 7-billion-parameter model on an old phone thinks about it, and a text-to-speech voice answers. All of it runs on hardware I own.

Whisper · ears Qwen 7B · brain Piper · voice

The walls I hit

  • 01
    The cable that only charged
    Lost an hour because a USB-C cable carried power but no data — the board looked dead. Learned the ESP32 download-mode trick: hold reset until the little green LED, then it flashes.
  • 02
    The board that quietly refused my WiFi
    My phone and Pi joined the network fine, so I almost blamed the chip. Turned out the access point defaulted to WPA3, which the ESP32 can't do — forcing WPA2 fixed it instantly.
  • 03
    My own network wouldn't let two devices talk
    The WiFi dongle isolates clients from each other, so the speaker and the brain couldn't reach one another. I bridged them with a tiny socat relay through the gateway. First relay I've ever built.
  • 04
    The wake word that went rogue
    Tried a "hey jarvis" hotword. It looped and started shouting responses to room noise. I rolled it back and wrote down exactly why it failed. Knowing when to stop is a skill too.
  • 05
    Audio that rasped
    The amp has no volume knob I could set in software, so loud meant distortion. Found the clean level by ear, locked it into the firmware, and pushed the fix over the air — no cable needed anymore.

What I picked up doing it

over-the-air firmware flashing editing Home Assistant storage by hand nmcli WiFi / AP config systemd services reading dmesg USB errors socat TCP relays how WPA2 vs WPA3 actually works
Where it landed

Live on my desk. Push-to-talk, fully local, zero cloud. The wake word is parked with notes for the next attempt — the rest of it just works, and I understand every piece of why.

Build log · 03

Auditing my own AI auditor

I want to use a local AI to help on authorized pentests. But putting your name on a job means knowing your tools' limits first — so before I trusted Ragnar, I made him take a test.

Two local models, six analyst tasks, one honest scorecard.

I ran the same battery — read scan output, draft findings, recall a CVE, give an exact command, and refuse to bluff — against a 7B (on an old phone) and a 3B (on a Pi). No cloud, no cheating.

What the test actually taught me

Where he's real
  • Reads tool output and prioritizes it sensibly
  • Drafts client-ready report text — genuinely his best skill
  • Wouldn't invent a Metasploit module for a fake CVE — he said "that's not real." Big trust signal.
Where he'll burn you
  • Handed me two nonexistent nmap scripts, sounding totally sure. Run it as-given → it errors.
  • Slow enough on little ARM boxes that a third of the tasks just timed out
  • The surprise: the smaller 3B wasn't faster — the Pi's CPU is weaker and already busy
The rule

An AI assistant is an accelerator, not an authority. Every command it gives and every CVE it names, I verify before it touches a client. That discipline is the job — and now I know exactly where Ragnar helps and where he'd get me burned. He rides the 7B; he's a decent junior analyst, never a source of truth.

Update · Jul

Living with him taught the sequel to that rule: right model, right moment. The 7B is the sharp one, but he's 5 GB on an 8 GB phone — leaving him loaded for every dashboard refresh pinned the whole box and the calls crawled to a timeout. So the routine background reads now run on the fast 3B, and the 7B only wakes when I actually summon him for a deep look. One model in memory at a time, never both — a strict hand-off, or the phone runs out of RAM and falls over. Same two models; the fix was scheduling them.

The method · how I verify

Talk is cheap; here's the actual discipline. Every claim a tool makes gets the same three steps before I trust it — Claim → Source of truth → Verdict. Two, worked from this audit:

Claim--script smb-enum-shares,nbtscan,smb-anon-check
Sourcenmap's own script folder — ls /usr/share/nmap/scripts/
Verdict2 of 3 scripts don't exist — the command errors as given. Real ones: smb-enum-users, nbstat.
Claim"CVE-2021-41773 affects Apache…"
Sourcenvd.nist.gov — the authoritative database
Verdictreal: Apache 2.4.49, path traversal, CVSS 9.8 — but the target ran 2.4.41, so it doesn't apply. Version is everything.
  1. Never report or run a claim I haven't checked myself.
  2. Every command → verify against the tool's own --help / man, or a lab VM first.
  3. Every CVE → look it up on NVD, and match the exact version.
  4. Every "fact" → cross-check one authoritative source, minimum.
  5. Can't verify it? It doesn't go in the report.
Build log · 05

Cloud vs Local AI — measured on my own fleet

Local AI isn't "worse cloud" — it's a different tool. So instead of guessing, I ran the same four prompts across my whole local ladder and a frontier cloud model, and timed and graded every answer.

1.5B, 3B, 7B on my own hardware — against cloud, on the same four prompts.

Factual, coding, a reasoning puzzle, and a pentest fact — timed wall-clock, graded right or wrong. The 1.5B told me nmap -sV means "Script Version" with total confidence. It doesn't; it's version detection.

1.5b · X1 (x86) 3b / 7b · the-one (ARM) cloud · claude CLI
The verdict

Easy tasks → the tiny model wins, it's fast and just as correct. Hard tasks → quality climbs with size, and cloud was right on all four while the local models fumbled reasoning or invented facts. Go cloud for the work that matters — code, pentest analysis, anything a wrong answer costs you. Keep local for the always-on house voice, quick offline commands, and anything private. Either way: verify, don't trust.
